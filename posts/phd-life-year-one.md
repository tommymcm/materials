# My PhD Experience

I'm getting to the end of my Ph.D. studies here at Northwestern University and am feeling a little sentimental.
I'd like to take a bit of time to talk about my path to this career path: how I got here, why I chose it, and my experiences along the way.
This time has been a combination of consistently amazing experiences with amazing people who I will always remember, along with rough patches that made me question if I'd make it to the end and doubt what things would look like when I got there.

I hope that this recounting is of some help to folks who are interested in pursuing a PhD, current students that are hitting a rough patch in their life and/or studies, or just interested in hearing details about what doing a PhD in the USA was like during the Covid-19 pandemic and the public funding fiasco that started in 2025.


## Why a PhD?

As I entered the final year of undergraduate studies at Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology, I found myself at an impasse.

For the longest time I had been training my sights on my dream job: Computer Architect at Texas Instruments working on Digital Signal Processors.
Ever since we had a section on the TI C6x in my intro to computer architecture class I was hooked.
Fom the technical elegance of software pipelining to the detailed report of how to manage people in such a massive co-designed project, I thought I had found the career and work environment of my dreams.
The summer between my third and fourth year, I got an internship working on the C7x (the successor of the C6x) and got to meet and work with so many wonderful people on truly interesting engineering problems.
But, I was miserable.
Something about the work-life balance and my tendency to isolate myself when given enough work to complete led to me struggling alone with no social life to speak of.

Luckily, I was about to start a new experience as a "Sophomore Resident Tutor" at Rose.
Looking back, nothing could have been better for me.
Immediately after seeing what a poor fit a technical industrial career was for me, I got to experience the joys of teaching in both group and one-on-one settings.
Even better, I lived in the sophomore residence halls and was able to be tightly integrate into the social life of the students I was tutoring.
I really enjoyed being someone that folks could go to for help on both their studies and life.

Because of this experience, I set my sights on a new career in teaching and advising.
I applied to schools (with absolutely no knowledge of what I was doing) and was luckily admitted to UIUC, Wisconsin-Madison and Northwestern.
The fit at UIUC was bad, so I was left to decide between Wisconsin-Madison and Northwestern.
I ended up flipping a coin at my kitchen table in the Spring of 2020 to decide between the two, so I landed on tails for Northwestern (which looking back, was the better choice).


## Year 1

For those who couldn't tell by the date above, I started my PhD right at the beginning of the Covid-19 pandemic.
This meant moving from Terre Haute, Indiana to Evanston, Illinois into an apartment that I had only seen grainy photos of online.
It was a terrible shoe box, but the least of my worries, because I was struggling with the transition.

Trying to meet my advisor, colleagues and cohort on zoom meetings (while dealing with a global pandemic) was not easy.
On top of this, I was essentially switching fields from Computer Engineering (with a focus on Computer Architecture) into Computer Science (with a focus on Compilers).
Not too big of a jump, but one that was difficult for someone fresh out of undergrad to manage.
After struggling to make much progress on my own research ideas, my advisor assigned me to an ongoing compiler-architecture co-design project to help out (given my background at TI).
Learning how to navigate a large research artifact was a great experience (and a good time sink compared to my other hobbies at the time of walking and binge-drinking alone in my room).
I started to catch a bit of a rhythm on coursework and that project, things were going well.

On November 8, 2020 my sister Katie passed away at the age of 29 from complications due to her abuse of alcohol.
Even today, I don't have words to express what it was like to lose Katie.
In lieu of that, I just wanted to restate the eulogy that I gave at her funeral:

> 
> For those of you that don't know me, I'm Tommy, I'm Chris and Blane's son and Katie was my sister.
> 
> For those of you that do know me, you'll know that growing up I was a pretty awkward and lonely kid.
> And for me, growing up was rough, but I always knew that Katie was there for me.
> She was more than just a sister you see at every holiday, she was one of my most enthusiastic supporters, someone who believed in me no matter what I wanted to do, and someone who knew just what to do or say to make my day better.
> I will always be thankful for the days that Katie spent taking me out to get wings with her and her friends, making me feel like I wasn't some nerd with no friends, but made me feel accepted and I can't really explain how much that meant to me at that time in my life.
> 
> I'll always remember her incredibly infectious personality.
> From the early mornings where she'd throw the towering pile of clothes in her back seat over to the other side and she'd drive me to school.
> I lost count of all the times she had to swerve away from some poor squirrel who decided to cross the road and she didn't see until the last second because she was too busy singing along to one of her favorite songs at the top of her lungs.
> Or hearing her struggle to hold back laughter while telling stories, like the time at Monical's pizza, where she'd stuff leftover pizza in her apron to snack on through her shift, and the fateful day where she accidentally dropped one of those slices onto the table while pulling out her notebook to take someone's order. 
> 
> Most importantly though, Katie was a role model to me.
> The way that I saw her talk about people, the admiration and pure joy in her voice was always something that amazed me about her.
> And the way that she could judge someone's character better than anyone I know, she could spot a fake person from a mile away and recognize a true friend in the most unlikely of places.

> And these words weren't being used to celebrate her life, but to celebrate the relationship that we shared.
> And I think more than anything that's what I regret.
> I never actually told Katie how much she meant to me and how much I admire her.
> Like most people in my family I did a lot of worrying about Katie.

> Enthusiastically support someone you care about, recklessly love those who need it, and always strive to make someone else laugh who needs to.

> I'd like to finish this off with an excerpt of one of my favorite poems by Mikko Harvey that has gained new meaning for me in these past couple weeks.

> The number of hours we have together is actually not so large.
